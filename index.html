Autofocus Complete!

  1. 1. The lens is out of position.

    When the lens is set for the wrong subject distance, light spreads across the sensor instead of meeting at one sharp point. In this simplified demo, the blur amount follows the absolute lens offset.

  2. 2. Special pixels see two views.

    In Dual Pixel PDAF, each split pixel receives light from opposite sides of the lens. When focus is off, the two views shift by a phase difference (Δ). This demo creates a simplified signal pair in code.

  3. 3. The camera calculates a correction.

    The camera slides one signal against the other and finds the best normalized correlation. The shift (Δ) estimates the direction and amount of defocus. Here, Cov means shared pattern and σ means signal spread.

  4. 4. The lens moves and the image sharpens.

    A real camera physically moves its lens until light meets sharply on the sensor; PDAF does not digitally remove blur. This demo uses xnext = x − g·e to move its virtual lens position toward zero.

This browser cannot read split-sensor pixels or move a real lens. Its two signals are made in code, and its CSS blur equals the absolute virtual lens offset.

03 / THE SCIENCE

What just
happened?

Let's break down the mechanism behind the experiment.

01

Light reaches the sensor

Light passes through the camera lens. If the lens is incorrectly positioned for the subject's distance, the image will not form sharply on the sensor plane.

02

The sensor compares two views

Split photodiodes receive light from different portions of the lens pupil. Their brightness patterns can be displaced when the image is out of focus.

03

Correlation estimates the shift

Cross-correlation tests possible alignments between the two signals. The alignment with the strongest match estimates the phase displacement.

04

The lens moves

A controller uses the estimated defocus to update the virtual lens position. It repeats until the remaining error is small.

THE CONTROL RULE

Correct a little. Measure again.

xnext = xcurrent − K × e

Here, e represents estimated defocus and K represents controller gain. This is a simplified proportional feedback model.

What this simulation does — and does not — claim

SplitSight extracts a brightness profile from the selected media, generates a paired signal with a controlled synthetic phase offset, estimates that offset using normalized cross-correlation and uses the estimate in a lens-position feedback loop. It is an educational model, not a physical Dual Pixel sensor emulator. Real cameras use calibrated sensor data, optical geometry, noise handling and more advanced control logic.

THE PROCESS, IN ORDER

How the pixels
are processed

The first stages process real pixels from the blurred canvas. This lesson intentionally skips character classification: the final result comes from the text you entered, not from a pixel-based OCR guess.

Pixels→ Gray→ Ink mask→ Regions→ Identified text
  1. Make a controlled image The browser draws your chosen text onto a canvas. The sharp and blurred previews show the actual generated pixels. Look at: sharp original → current blurred input.
  2. Turn colour into brightness Each pixel has red, green and blue values. We calculate Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B. Y is brightness; the weights reflect how strongly each colour contributes to perceived light. Why: one brightness number is simpler to compare than three colour channels.
  3. Reduce speckles and separate ink An optional 3 × 3 median filter replaces each pixel with the middle of nine nearby brightness values, which can remove isolated specks. Then a global cutoff or nearby-pixel average classifies pixels as ink or background. Look at: grayscale/filter and black-and-white threshold canvases.
  4. Group dark pixels into regions Connected ink pixels form components. The program groups components into lines and estimates character boxes from their positions and gaps. Touching or broken letters can confuse these estimates. Look at: the coloured boxes drawn on the detected-characters canvas.
  5. What a real OCR would do next A real OCR system would decide which letter each boxed shape resembles. This walkthrough deliberately skips that classification step. Important: boxes show regions only; they do not identify letters.
  6. Show the identified text The result shown below is copied from the text entered to make this image. It is not identified from the pixels; this lesson deliberately skips character recognition. Result: displayed from your input; it is not an OCR prediction.
